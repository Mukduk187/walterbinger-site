What I actually do

Most operational problems don't begin with lazy people or bad intentions. They begin when the plan on paper no longer matches the reality on the ground. That's usually where I come in.

I listen to the people doing the work. I find where information, responsibility, and trust are breaking down. Then I help build clearer workflows, stronger managers, and systems people can actually follow when the pressure rises.

I tend to be most useful when:

  • a new account or service is struggling to stabilize,
  • a team has lost confidence in the process,
  • growth has outpaced the existing structure,
  • leadership knows something is wrong but can't yet see the whole system,
  • or a rollout is technically complete but adoption has stalled.
A story instead of a slogan
Deer Isle, Maine · 2020–2021

I entered long-term care just as COVID arrived. When the outbreak reached our nursing home, staff got sick, equipment failed, call bells multiplied, and every decision carried consequences no operating manual had prepared us for.

I worked eighteen-hour shifts, crossed departmental lines, did infection-control barrier work and resident and post-mortem care, and watched good people try to deliver humane care inside a system collapsing around them. When the facility finally closed, I helped safely relocate roughly seventy residents.

That year changed how I understand leadership. A system isn't successful because it works in ideal conditions. It's successful because people can still understand and use it when conditions stop being ideal.

The pattern across my career

My work has moved through several industries, but the job has stayed surprisingly consistent.

Healthcare

Led district operations across eight skilled-nursing and rehabilitation facilities — stabilizing newly awarded accounts, developing managers, improving regulatory readiness, and keeping teams delivering through disruption.

Hospitality & entrepreneurship

Built and ran restaurants — including a Lower East Side empanada shop and a Buenos Aires restaurant-bar operated entirely in Spanish — and learned that a technically correct plan is worthless when the people expected to execute it don't trust or understand it.

Implementation & systems

Introduced new tools, rebuilt workflows, wrote operating procedures, trained leaders, and translated executive expectations into practical steps that made sense on the floor — treating errors as signals for where to improve, not who to blame.

Building the whole machine

Owning ventures taught me to see it all at once: customer experience, staffing, cash flow, physical space, process design, communication, and the thousand small failures that eventually become one large problem.

The principle

Understand the problem. Earn trust quickly. Build systems people will use. Leave the operation stronger than you found it.

That's the thread connecting every role I've held.
